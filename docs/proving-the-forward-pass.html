Step 1 of 9

The job: prove "I ran M on x and got y"

Alice runs a datacenter. Bob is the inspector. Alice says: "I ran model M on input x, with random seed r, and the output was y." Bob wants to be sure. Two things make that hard.

Alice won't show Bob the weights. They're the most valuable thing she owns. And Bob can't re-run every request himself. He'd need a datacenter as big as hers, and he'd need the weights anyway.

A zero-knowledge proof is a file Alice produces that gives Bob three guarantees at once.

  • CompleteIf Alice is honest, Bob's check always passes.
  • SoundIf Alice cheats, Bob's check fails, except with a probability so small it never happens in practice (about one in 2100).
  • Zero-knowledgeBob learns nothing beyond "the statement is true." Not the weights, not other users' inputs.

Whether this is usable depends on three costs. Prover time: how much slower than plain inference. Proof size: what Alice ships to Bob. Verifier time: what Bob spends. The whole story of the last two years is those three numbers coming down.

Hold onto this: the proof says y = F(W, x, r) for a committed W. Everything below is about how you can check that equation without seeing W and without recomputing F.
Step 2 of 9

Convinced without being shown

Start with the oldest teaching example, because the real thing works the same way. Alice claims two balls are different colors. Bob can't see color. Bob hides the balls behind his back, either swaps them or doesn't, shows them again, and asks: "Did I swap?"

If the balls really differ, Alice always answers correctly. If they're identical, she's guessing, and each round she survives with a 50% chance. After twenty rounds a bluffer survives one time in a million. Bob never learns which color is which, and he ends up sure anyway.

Step 3 of 9

Sealed envelopes: hashes as commitments

Challenges only work if Alice locks in her claim before she sees the challenge. Otherwise she adapts her story. The lock is a cryptographic hash: a function that turns any input into a fixed 256-bit fingerprint.

A hash gives two things at once. It's binding: nobody can find a second input with the same fingerprint, so Alice can't swap contents later. It's hiding: the fingerprint reveals nothing about the contents. Publish the hash now, that's the commitment. Reveal the contents later, that's the opening. Anyone can check they match.

Change one character and watch the fingerprint change completely.

Bench: SHA-256, live in your browser

Takeaway: "commit, then get challenged, then open" is the rhythm of every proof below. The hash is what stops Alice rewriting history.
Step 4 of 9

Committing to 60 GB without revealing it: Merkle trees

One hash of the whole model is a fine commitment, but opening it means revealing the whole model. Bob only ever needs to look at tiny pieces. A Merkle tree fixes this: chop the weights into chunks, hash each chunk, hash pairs of hashes, and repeat up to a single root.

The root commits to every chunk. To open one chunk, Alice reveals that chunk plus the sibling hash at each level up the tree. Bob rehashes his way to the root and compares. For a 31B model in 1 MB chunks that's about 60,000 chunks and a path of 16 hashes, half a kilobyte, to prove any one of them.

Click a chunk to see its opening path. Then tamper with one and watch the root change.

Takeaway: the model's identity, for the rest of this walkthrough, is one 32-byte root. "The committed model was used" means "every chunk Alice opened hashed up to this root."
Step 5 of 9

A model as equations

A proof system can only talk about arithmetic: additions and multiplications over a finite field, meaning integers modulo a large prime. That sounds restrictive until you notice what a transformer's forward pass mostly is: matrix multiplies. Each output number is one equation, y_i = Σ_j W_ij · x_j. A whole layer is a few million of those.

Two things don't fit. Softmax, GELU, and RMSNorm aren't polynomials, so provers handle them with lookup tables: precompute the function on every allowed input, commit to the table, and prove each (input, output) pair appears in it. And floating point doesn't live in a finite field, so the matmuls are quantized to integers. Attestable uses 8-bit integers for matmuls and keeps the non-linear parts in floating point, which is where its small accuracy loss comes from.

Step 6 of 9

Checking billions of equations with one random number

Here's the trick that makes verification cheap. Pack a huge pile of equations into polynomials. Then use one fact from algebra: two different polynomials of degree d can agree on at most d points. Over a field with 261 elements, if Bob picks a random point and the two sides agree there, a cheat slipped through with probability at most d / 261.

So Bob doesn't check the equations. He asks Alice to commit to her polynomials (Merkle roots again), picks a random point, asks her to open the values at that point (Merkle paths again), and checks the relation holds at just that point. Modern systems layer this idea into the sum-check protocol and FRI-style low-degree tests, but the core is right here.

Try it in a toy field of 101 elements. Alice's fake polynomial has been crafted to agree with the honest one at three points, the most a degree-3 difference allows.

Step 7 of 9

Sending Bob home: Fiat-Shamir

Everything so far needs Bob in the room, picking random points after each commitment. That's useless for a proof you want to file and check next year. The Fiat-Shamir transform removes him: Alice computes each "random" challenge as the hash of everything she has committed so far.

She can't steer that hash. Changing any commitment to get a friendlier challenge changes the hash, which changes the challenge. The result is a single file Alice can produce alone that anyone can verify later, and the verifier just recomputes the same hashes to confirm the challenges were derived honestly.

The price is an assumption: the hash has to behave like a truly random function. That is the assumption Yogi named as the one his team trusts least, and why he mentioned falling back to an interactive protocol if needed.

Takeaway: a "proof" in this field is a file, checkable by anyone, any time. The file's security rests on hash functions behaving randomly, which is also why it's post-quantum and needs no trusted setup.
Step 8 of 9

The whole pipeline for one forward pass

Now assemble the pieces. This is roughly what a hash-based, STARK-style prover for LLM inference does.

Three things explain why it took until 2026 to be usable. Proving used to be thousands of times slower than inference, so the systems that matter are the ones that run the prover on the same GPUs as the model. Lookup arguments got cheap enough to cover the non-linear layers. And hash-based (STARK-style) proofs, with no trusted setup and no elliptic curves, turned out to parallelize well on GPUs.

Step 9 of 9

What the proof does not say

The proof establishes exactly one sentence: this committed model, on this input, with this seed, produced this output. It's a proof of correctness for a declared workload. It says nothing about what else the GPUs did that hour. A datacenter can produce perfect proofs for its public inference and run a training job on the side.

That missing half is called completeness, and it's the gap the whole group argues about. The proposals fall into three families, and they stack.

Two more limits to keep in mind. The proof binds to a committed model, so someone still has to check that root(W) is the model everyone agreed on, which is a governance problem. And the proof is only as trustworthy as its implementation, which is why Attestable's Lean proof of soundness, and the argument about publishing the prover code, matter as much as the benchmarks.

Takeaway: ZK proofs are the one component in the stack that gives a mathematical guarantee with no trusted hardware. They answer "was this output computed correctly?" They do not answer "was that all you computed?"

What we built: receipts and a recompute verifier

The nine steps above are the ZK path. What exists in code today is the rung below it: every answer from llama.cpp comes with a signed receipt, and a second machine holding the same model file recomputes the answer and compares. No proof, no cryptographic circuit. Just commitments, a replayable sampler, and a careful comparison.

The honest limit, stated up front: the verifier needs the weights. It re-runs the model. That covers open-weight models served by providers, a lab auditing its own inference, and an auditor trusted with weights. It does not cover a closed frontier model whose owner won't share them. That case is what the ZK path is for.

Two design choices carry the whole thing. The sampler is replayable by construction: the uniform draw at position t is a hash of the seed and t, ties break by token id, and truncation happens in float64, so any machine reproduces the exact draw with no RNG state. And the verifier records the logits hash at every position, which tells it whether it's looking at bit-identical arithmetic or a different backend. That distinction turned out to matter more than anything else.

Takeaway: this rung answers "did this model, with these settings, produce these tokens?" for anyone who can hold the model. Receipts with re-run checks exist elsewhere (INVAR for Ollama, EigenAI, CommitLLM), and tolerant replay exists (Token-DiFR, Rinberg et al.). What's new here is the combination: logits hashes in the receipt choosing a strict or tolerant rule, and hash-derived draws that make the gap exact. It's also the base the openings and proof rungs plug into.

Drift or tamper? The draw gap

The first version of the verifier counted token matches and log-prob differences, and it got fooled in both directions. Honest cross-backend replays failed (Metal and CPU disagree on Q4_K_M arithmetic), and a wrong-seed forgery passed on peaked prompts (same model, same logits, log-prob difference exactly zero).

The fix is one number per mismatch. Under the verifier's distribution, every token owns a slice of the unit interval. The receipt says which draw u was used and which token was picked. If u falls inside the claimed token's slice, the replay agrees. If it falls just outside, the boundary moved a little: backend drift. If it falls far away, the prover's distribution or its draw was something else: tamper. That distance is the draw gap.

The real verifier applies this at every position. Same backend: logits hashes all match, so any mismatch at all is rejected. Different backend: it accepts if the largest gap stays under 0.45 and the mean gap over all positions stays under 0.03, numbers calibrated on the honest runs in part 3.
Takeaway: hashes of the logits tell you whether the arithmetic was identical. Only replaying the seeded sampler tells you whether the sampling was honest, and the draw gap is what separates a moved boundary from a moved draw.

Results and limits

95 of 96 verdicts correct. Three lessons came out of the data rather than the plan.

1Same-backend replay is bit-exact. Across runs and thread counts, the logits hash matches at every position. So the verifier has two regimes: when hashes all match, any mismatch is tamper; when they don't, small gaps are drift.
2Logit hashes say nothing about the sampler. Temperature, greedy, and seed tampering all leave the logits bit-identical. Replaying the seeded sampler is the only thing that catches them.
3Cross-backend drift on quantized models is large. Metal against CPU on Q4_K_M moved single draws by 0.43 of the unit interval. The drift thresholds are fitted to eight prompts and the margin is thin. Verify on the same backend when you can; when you can't, use longer sequences so systematic shifts separate from symmetric noise.

Glossary

Words you'll meet in the group

ZKP
Zero-knowledge proof. Proves a statement is true without revealing why.
Prover / verifier
Alice and Bob. The datacenter and whoever is checking it.
Soundness
A false statement can't get an accepting proof, except with negligible probability. Attestable proved this property of their protocol in Lean.
Commitment
A hash published before a challenge, so the contents can't change afterwards.
Merkle root
The single hash at the top of a tree of hashes. Commits to a huge dataset; any piece can be opened with a short path.
Finite field
Integers modulo a large prime. The arithmetic every proof system speaks.
Arithmetization
Turning a computation into equations over a field.
Lookup argument
A way to prove a value is in a pre-committed table. How softmax and GELU get proven.
Sum-check
A protocol for proving a giant sum is correct by checking polynomials at random points.
STARK
A hash-based proof system. No trusted setup, post-quantum, GPU-friendly. Attestable's is in this family.
Fiat-Shamir
Turning an interactive protocol into a standalone proof by deriving challenges from hashes.
Correctness vs completeness
"The declared work was done right" vs "nothing else was done." ZK gives the first. Exhaustion, taps, and compartmentalization go after the second.
Resource exhaustion
Filling idle GPU capacity with provable dummy work so no room is left for covert training.
Recomputation (DiFR, TOPLOC)
The non-ZK alternative: the verifier re-runs a random sample of requests on trusted hardware and compares. Needs the weights or a trusted copy.
